# SEM and EDS

The scanning electron microscope is the most heavily used instrument in this course, and often the first microscope any thin film sees. It images topography from millimeters down to about a nanometer, adds compositional contrast and elemental analysis with almost no sample preparation, and does it all quickly enough to survey many samples in an afternoon. This page covers image formation, the interaction volume that governs both resolution and analysis, and X-ray microanalysis by energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS).

| At a glance | |
| --- | --- |
| Probe in / signal out | Focused keV electron beam in; secondary electrons, backscattered electrons, and X-rays out |
| Information | Surface morphology, Z contrast, elemental composition (EDS), grain orientation (EBSD) |
| Depth probed | Nanometers (SE) to micrometers (BSE, X-rays), set by beam voltage |
| Lateral resolution | About 1 nm (SE imaging); about 1 um for EDS in bulk samples |
| Sensitivity | EDS detects about 0.1 to 1 wt% for most elements |
| Sample requirements | Vacuum compatible; insulators need coating or low-voltage operation; nondestructive |

## Image formation

An SEM focuses electrons, typically 0.5 to 30 keV from a field emission source, into a probe of about a nanometer, scans it in a raster, and plots a detected signal versus probe position. There is no imaging lens after the sample; magnification is purely the ratio of display to scan size, which is why SEM combines enormous depth of field with a magnification range spanning five orders. Resolution is set by the probe size and by how far the detected signal spreads inside the sample.

The two workhorse signals were introduced with the [electron-solid physics](../espec/electron-solid.md):

- **Secondary electrons** escape from only a few nanometers' depth, so they map topography: edges and tilted surfaces emit more (the edge effect), giving the intuitive three-dimensional look of SEM images. The in-lens detectors of modern instruments collect SE generated by the unscattered probe, giving the sharpest surface detail.
- **Backscattered electrons** carry compositional contrast, with yield rising monotonically with mean atomic number. A BSE image of a polished cross-section maps phase distribution with no etching, and at low kV becomes remarkably surface sensitive.

The **interaction volume** ties everything together. The beam spreads into a pear-shaped region whose size scales roughly as $E^{1.7}/\rho$, from tens of nanometers at 1 kV to several micrometers at 30 kV. Signals escape from different fractions of it: SE from the top few nanometers, BSE from a substantial fraction, X-rays from nearly all of it. Beam voltage is therefore the most important knob on the instrument. For thin films the modern trend to low-voltage imaging (below about 2 kV) is decisive: it confines the interaction volume to the film rather than the substrate, reveals true surface structure, and controls charging on insulators by balancing electron input against emission.

## Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy

The characteristic X-rays generated in the interaction volume, identified by the same core-level physics as [XPS lines](../espec/xps.md), are collected by a silicon drift detector that resolves them by energy into a full spectrum at every probe position. EDS gives elemental identification of features in seconds and elemental maps in minutes, and is the reason an SEM session so often answers "what is this made of" on the spot.

Its limits come from the physics already in hand. X-rays escape from the whole micrometer-scale interaction volume, so EDS spatial resolution in bulk samples is about a micrometer regardless of probe size, and a thin film thinner than the interaction volume yields a spectrum dominated by substrate; low kV helps but restricts which lines can be excited. Energy resolution of about 125 eV leaves peak overlaps that software must deconvolve. Light elements suffer from the [fluorescence yield](../espec/electron-solid.md) branching against X-ray emission, making EDS quantification below about Z of 11 semiquantitative at best; boron, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen problems frequently belong to AES, XPS, or EELS instead. Standards-based quantification with matrix (ZAF) corrections on flat polished samples reaches a few percent relative accuracy, and standardless quantification, the one-click default, should be treated as an estimate.

Wavelength dispersive spectrometers (WDS), which diffract X-rays from analyzer crystals, improve energy resolution by an order of magnitude and detection limits to about 0.01 wt%; an electron probe microanalyzer is an SEM built around several of them, and remains the standard for accurate quantitative microanalysis of bulk specimens.

## SEM practice for thin films

The recurring uses in film work: plan-view grain structure imaging, fracture cross-sections for quick thickness and morphology (with the caveat that fracture surfaces follow weak paths), step-coverage inspection of conformal coatings, and defect review. For quantitative grain size and texture the SEM hands off to [EBSD](ebsd-fib.md), and for interfaces sharper than the interaction volume permits, to [STEM cross-sections](../stem/stem-imaging.md).
