# SEM and EDS

The scanning electron microscope is the most heavily used instrument in this course, and often the first microscope any thin film sees. It images topography from millimeters down to about a nanometer, adds compositional contrast and elemental analysis with almost no sample preparation, and does it all quickly enough to survey many samples in an afternoon. This page covers the instrument, image formation, the interaction volume that governs both resolution and analysis, and X-ray microanalysis by energy dispersive spectroscopy (EDS).

| At a glance | |
| --- | --- |
| Probe in / signal out | Focused keV electron beam in; secondary electrons, backscattered electrons, and X-rays out |
| Information | Surface morphology, Z contrast, elemental composition (EDS), grain orientation (EBSD) |
| Depth probed | Nanometers (SE) to micrometers (BSE, X-rays), set by beam voltage |
| Lateral resolution | About 1 nm (SE imaging); about 1 um for EDS in bulk samples |
| Sensitivity | EDS detects about 0.1 to 1 wt% for most elements |
| Sample requirements | Vacuum compatible; insulators need coating or low-voltage operation; nondestructive |

## The instrument

{ "name": "sem" }
:::

An SEM is an electron gun, two or three demagnifying lenses, scan coils, and detectors. The gun choice has large consequences, because it sets the **brightness** (current per area per solid angle), the conserved quantity that limits how much current fits into a small probe. Thermionic tungsten and LaB$_6$ guns are robust and cheap; field-emission guns (cold or Schottky) are orders of magnitude brighter with a much smaller energy spread, and every high-resolution or low-voltage instrument uses one. The electron optics demagnify the source into a probe whose diameter is a competition between the source image, diffraction at the aperture, spherical aberration ($\propto \alpha^3$), and chromatic aberration ($\propto \Delta E/E$, the term that dominates at low beam energy and makes the FEG's small energy spread decisive there). The practical consequences follow directly: there is an optimum aperture angle, resolution worsens as beam energy drops, and probe current trades against probe size, so imaging and microanalysis prefer different settings on the same instrument.

The image itself is built serially: the probe raster-scans, a detector signal is recorded at each position, and magnification is nothing but the ratio of display size to scanned size, which is why SEM magnification is continuous over five orders and why the depth of field (set by the tiny beam convergence angle, milliradians) is so much larger than in light microscopy.

## Image formation and contrast

The two workhorse signals were introduced with the [electron-solid physics](../espec/electron-solid.md), and their different escape depths explain their different images:

- **Secondary electrons** escape from only a few nanometers' depth. Their yield rises steeply with the tilt of the local surface (a tilted surface puts more of the generation volume within escape range) and at edges, which escape from two sides: this edge effect plus tilt contrast is the familiar three-dimensional look of SEM images. The standard Everhart-Thornley detector sits to one side with a small positive bias that collects even secondaries emitted away from it, adding a gentle directional shading that reads like illumination. It is worth distinguishing SE1 (generated by the incident probe at the impact point, carrying the high-resolution information) from SE2 (generated by backscattered electrons exiting far from the impact point, carrying blurrier, more material-dependent contrast); in-lens detectors on modern instruments favor SE1 and give the sharpest surface detail.
- **Backscattered electrons** carry compositional contrast, with the backscatter coefficient rising monotonically with mean atomic number. A BSE image of a polished cross-section maps phase distribution with no etching, and annular solid-state or in-column BSE detectors separate this compositional signal from topography by symmetry (topography shows up in the difference between detector segments, composition in their sum).

The **interaction volume** ties everything together. The beam spreads into a pear-shaped region whose size scales roughly as $E^{1.7}/\rho$ (the Kanaya-Okayama range), from tens of nanometers at 1 kV to several micrometers at 30 kV, and the simulation below computes it live for real materials. Signals escape from different fractions of it: SE from the top few nanometers, BSE from a substantial fraction of the range, X-rays from nearly all of it. Beam voltage is therefore the most important knob on the instrument. For thin films the modern trend to low-voltage imaging (below about 2 kV) is decisive: it confines the interaction volume to the film rather than the substrate, reveals true surface structure rather than a translucent average, and manages charging on insulators, because at low energy the total emitted-electron yield can reach unity and the surface self-stabilizes near the second crossover energy, letting uncoated insulators be imaged with patience and the right voltage.

The simulation below computes real electron trajectories with the standard single-scattering Monte Carlo model (screened Rutherford elastic scattering plus Bethe energy loss). Backscattered trajectories are highlighted, and the backscatter yield $\eta$ accumulates live. Three experiments to run: sweep the beam energy on silicon and watch the interaction volume grow as roughly $E^{1.7}$; hold the energy fixed and step through the targets to watch the volume shrink and the backscatter fraction climb with atomic number; then set a finite sample thickness and sweep the energy again. With the view locked to the film, the deposited energy moves from fully contained to punching through as the range passes the thickness, which is the geometry of every thin film measured on a substrate and of every electron-transparent lamella. All three trends are the daily working intuition of SEM operation.

## Energy dispersive X-ray spectroscopy

The characteristic X-rays generated in the interaction volume are collected by a silicon drift detector (SDD): each photon absorbed in the silicon creates electron-hole pairs in proportion to its energy, and the pulse-height spectrum is the X-ray spectrum. Line energies follow Moseley's law, increasing systematically with atomic number, which is why an EDS spectrum reads directly as an element list, and why neighboring elements produce neighboring lines. Detector energy resolution, limited by carrier statistics to roughly 125 eV at Mn K$\alpha$, leaves specific well-known overlaps (Si K with W M and Ta M; S K with Mo L and Pb M; N K with Ti L) that software deconvolution handles only partly, and that a wavelength-dispersive spectrometer resolves outright. EDS gives elemental identification of features in seconds and elemental maps in minutes, which is why an SEM session can usually identify what a feature is made of immediately.

Its limits come from the physics already in hand. X-rays escape from the whole micrometer-scale interaction volume, so EDS spatial resolution in bulk samples is about a micrometer regardless of probe size, and a thin film thinner than the interaction volume yields a spectrum dominated by substrate; dropping the beam voltage helps, but each line needs an overvoltage of two to three times its excitation energy, so low-kV analysis must switch to lower-energy lines (L and M instead of K) with their own overlap problems. Light elements suffer doubly, from the [fluorescence-yield branching](../espec/electron-solid.md) against X-ray emission and from absorption of their soft X-rays on the way out, making EDS quantification below about Z of 11 semiquantitative at best; boron, carbon, nitrogen, and oxygen problems usually belong to AES, XPS, or EELS instead. Quantification on flat polished bulk samples ratios each line to a standard and applies matrix corrections for atomic number, absorption, and secondary fluorescence (the ZAF scheme), reaching a few percent relative accuracy; standardless quantification, the one-click default on every instrument, should be treated as an estimate. And thin-film or rough-sample geometries violate the flat-bulk assumptions of ZAF entirely, which is where dedicated thin-film EDS models or a switch to [STEM-EDS](../stem/analytical-stem.md) become necessary.

Wavelength dispersive spectrometers (WDS), which diffract the X-rays from analyzer crystals and count one narrow band at a time, improve energy resolution by an order of magnitude and detection limits to about 0.01 wt%; an electron probe microanalyzer (EPMA), colloquially the microprobe, is an SEM built around several of them with regulated beam current, and remains the standard for accurate quantitative microanalysis of bulk specimens.

## SEM practice for thin films

The recurring uses in film work: plan-view grain structure imaging (low kV, in-lens detector), fracture cross-sections for quick thickness and morphology (with the caveat that fracture follows weak paths and the measured thickness includes fracture artifacts), step-coverage inspection of conformal coatings over topography, particle and defect review, and voltage-contrast inspection of electrically floating versus grounded features. For quantitative grain size and texture the SEM hands off to [EBSD](ebsd-fib.md), and for interfaces sharper than the interaction volume permits, to [STEM cross-sections](../stem/stem-imaging.md).
